Atomic Habits Section III • The 3rd Law

Disciplined People Are Better at Structuring Their Environment

In 1971, as the Vietnam War was heading into its sixteenth year, congressmen Robert Steele and Morgan Murphy discovered something astonishing: over 15 percent of American soldiers stationed in Vietnam were regular heroin users. Subsequent research showed that 35 percent of enlisted men had tried heroin, and as many as 20 percent were clinically addicted.

The discovery led to a flurry of panic in Washington, including the creation of the Special Action Office of Drug Abuse Prevention under President Nixon, tasked with tracking and rehabilitating soldiers upon their return home.

“When soldiers who had been addicted to heroin returned to the United States, only 5 percent became re-addicted within a year, and just 12 percent relapsed within three years.”

In other words, approximately nine out of ten soldiers who used heroin in Vietnam were cured virtually overnight. They didn’t undergo sheer willpower exercises or moral reformation; their entire cue environment had radically transformed.

Chapter 7

The Secret to Self-Control

T he Robins study contradicted many of our cultural beliefs about bad habits. We have been taught that unhealthy habits are moral weaknesses. If you are overweight or a smoker or an addict, you’ve been told your entire life that it is because you lack self-discipline.

Yet when scientists analyze people who appear to have tremendous willpower, it turns out those individuals aren’t all that different from those who are struggling. Instead, “disciplined” people are simply better at structuring their lives in a way that does not require heroic willpower and self-control.

Habit Architecture Metric
Resisting Temptation (Willpower Tax) High Friction • 12% Success
Removing Tempting Cues (Environment) Zero Friction • 88% Success

In short, they spend less time in tempting situations. The people with the greatest self-control are typically the ones who need to use it the least.
